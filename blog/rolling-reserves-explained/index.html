Rolling Reserves Explained: How They Work and How to Get Your Money Back

You've found a payment provider that accepts your pharmacy or supplement shop. Then you read the contract and see it: "rolling reserve: 10% for 180 days."

It sounds like they're keeping your money. In a way, they are, but only temporarily. Here's how reserves work, why you're being asked for one, and how to get better terms.

What is a rolling reserve?

A rolling reserve is a safety buffer. Your payment provider keeps back a small percentage of each day's sales for a set period, then releases it to you.

A simple example:

  • You sell €1,000 on 1 March
  • The reserve is 10% for 180 days
  • You receive €900 straight away (minus normal fees)
  • The other €100 is released to you about 180 days later, in late August

The "rolling" part means this happens every day. After the first few months, money is being released to you every day as well as held back, so it becomes a steady cycle rather than a growing pile.

Why do providers ask for a reserve?

When a customer disputes a card payment, the money goes back to the customer, and the payment provider has to cover it first. If your business closed or couldn't pay, the provider would lose that money.

Health, pharmacy and supplement businesses are seen as higher risk because of:

  • Disputes that arrive months later. Customers can often dispute a card payment many weeks after the purchase.
  • Subscriptions, where forgotten repeat charges lead to disputes
  • Regulatory changes that could affect your products overnight
  • Industry history. A few bad actors make everyone in the category look riskier.

The reserve means the provider has something to fall back on. For you, it's often the price of getting approved at all.

What's typical?

Terms vary a lot by provider and business, but commonly:

  • Percentage: often around 5% to 10% of sales
  • Holding period: often 90 to 180 days

New businesses, businesses with no processing history, and those in categories like CBD or subscriptions usually start at the higher end.

Other types of reserve you may see

  • Upfront (capped) reserve: a fixed amount held at the start, instead of a percentage of each sale
  • Capped rolling reserve: a percentage is held until the total reaches an agreed cap, then it stops
  • Reserve after closure: when an account is closed, providers often hold the balance for months to cover late disputes

Ask which type you're being offered. A cap is almost always better for you than an open-ended percentage.

How to get a better deal

Reserves are more negotiable than most people think, especially if you prepare.

1. Show your processing history.

Six to twelve months of clean statements with low disputes is your strongest argument.

2. Show your dispute and refund rates.

If you can show disputes well below 1% of transactions, you're a lower risk than the provider assumes.

3. Explain your business model clearly.

A licensed pharmacy with prescription checks and clear refund policies looks very different from an anonymous webshop. Lead with your licence.

4. Ask for a review date.

Get it written into your contract: "Reserve to be reviewed after 6 months of processing." Then put the date in your diary.

5. Ask for a cap.

"10% until €10,000 is held" is much easier to live with than "10% forever".

6. Plan your cash flow for it.

If you know you'll get a 10% reserve for 180 days, factor it into your planning from month one. It hurts most when it's a surprise.

How to get held funds released

If money is being held longer than you expected:

  • Check your contract for the release schedule
  • Ask in writing for a statement of how much is held and when each amount will be released
  • Keep handling refunds properly. New disputes can extend the hold
  • Ask for a partial release if your disputes have stayed low. Providers can agree to this, but only if you ask

This article is general information, not financial or legal advice. Reserve terms depend on your provider and contract.

Frequently asked questions

Is a reserve a sign that the provider doesn't trust me?

Not really. It's standard in higher-risk categories. Think of it like a deposit on a rental: normal, temporary and refundable.

Do I earn interest on my reserve?

Usually not. Check your contract.

Can I avoid a reserve completely?

Sometimes, with a strong processing history in a lower-risk category. For cannabis, CBD or subscription-heavy businesses, a small reserve is common. The goal is fair terms and a clear path to reducing them.

What happens to my reserve if I switch providers?

It's released on the original schedule, not immediately. Plan for that when you move.
